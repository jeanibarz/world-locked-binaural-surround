I'm Chasing a $4,000 Surround Rig's Feel in Software — With Just a Webcam

Put on headphones and play anything — a film, a game, a track. The sound sits in a line between your ears, stuck inside your head. Even "surround" and "spatial audio" modes mostly just smear that line a little wider. Real speakers feel nothing like that: they feel like they're out in the room. The paid way to get that feel on headphones is a multi-thousand-dollar hardware rig like the Smyth Realiser. I've been building a renderer to chase the same out-in-the-room feel in software, with no special hardware — just your webcam.

▶ [ Embedded video — ~2½-minute explainer ]
Short version: the engine runs on Windows today; whether world-locking actually gets sound out of your head is the hypothesis I'm now testing with real listeners. Below, I go a little deeper than the video does.

First, why headphones trap sound in your head

Plain stereo controls exactly one thing: how loud each ear is. That's enough to slide a sound left or right, but it can't place it in front of you, behind you, or above — so everything collapses onto a line running ear to ear, inside your skull. That's the baseline most people live with, and it's why "surround" on stock headphones underwhelms: panning a sound wider isn't the same as putting it out in the room.

Binaural: give each ear what it would really hear

In a real room your ears get far more than loudness. A source reaches one ear slightly earlier and slightly louder, and the shape of your head and outer ears stamps a direction-dependent "tonal fingerprint" onto it. Audio engineers bundle all of that into one filter — your Head-Related Transfer Function (HRTF). Recreate what each ear would actually receive, and you get binaural audio: now a sound can sit to your left, in front, or overhead, and it starts to feel like it's out of your head rather than pasted between your ears. This is the step "spatial audio" is built on.

The catch binaural can't fix on its own: you turn your head

Here's the problem. A binaural scene is computed for one fixed head position. Turn your head and the whole scene turns with you — the voice that was dead ahead is suddenly off to your left. Real rooms never behave that way: the speakers stay where they are while you move, and your brain leans hard on that steadiness. Look at a mug on your desk and sway your head — it holds its place because it's really out there; a smudge on your glasses travels with you because it isn't. Your ears run the same test. A sound that stays put as you move reads as real; a sound that rotates with your head gets filed back under "inside my head." That missing cue — stability under your own motion — is exactly where head-tracking comes in.

The fix: lock the speakers to the world

Head-tracking is the enabler. Because a webcam follows where your head is pointing in real time, the renderer can keep a full virtual 7.1 set (seven speakers around you plus a subwoofer) world-locked — fixed to your room, not your head. When you turn, the speakers don't move; only the calculation that renders them to your two ears changes. The gunfire stays where it happened, and your ears are what moved, not the room. The bet is that restoring that stability-under-motion cue is what finally keeps the sound out in the room — the hypothesis this whole project is built to test, not yet a measured result.

No special hardware — just your webcam

The tracker is a face-landmark model reading yaw, pitch and roll — how far you turn, tilt and nod — straight from the webcam, reliably across about 45° to either side, the range you actually use glancing between screens. Many times a second, the engine computes each virtual speaker's direction relative to your current pose and runs it through the matching ear filter (its impulse response) for that angle. Turn your head; the render follows, block by block. Using a commodity webcam as a head-orientation (3-DoF) tracker — instead of a $200 motion-sensor (IMU) headset — is the part I'm most happy with.

What works today — and the one thing I haven't proven yet

Running end-to-end on Windows right now: a world-locked 7.1 stage, webcam head-tracking, a native Rust DSP (digital signal processing) core that runs ~5× faster than real time on an ordinary CPU with no graphics card, a live parametric equalizer (per-band tone correction) and a personal headphone correction, all driven from one cockpit. On the plumbing: any app plays into VB-Audio Cable (the virtual audio device that ships with VoiceMeeter), and the engine outputs through your normal Windows sound device — no custom driver. Next: fine-tuning those ear filters toward your own ears, presets, and an AI "judge" — a synthetic listener — that scores a calibration consistently instead of leaning on an unreliable ear. The aspiration is the out-in-the-room feel of a multi-thousand-dollar hardware rig like the Smyth Realiser, in software, on the headphones you already own. To be straight: that out-of-head benefit is the hypothesis I'm testing, not a shipped result.
